Sender identity verification project

What a Universal Identity certificate does to AI bot traffic

The same certificate architecture built to stop spoofed calls and emails also gives networks a way to tell a verified human or disclosed-bot sender from an anonymous automated one — before that traffic ever reaches you.

The gap today

Automated senders — AI-driven call bots, message bots, and content-generating accounts — currently look identical to legitimate traffic at the network level. There's no equivalent of a "return address" that a receiver, ISP, or platform can check before deciding whether to let a bot-originated message or call through. A Universal Identity certificate closes that gap by attaching a verifiable, revocable identity claim to the sender, and by requiring that claim to disclose whether the sender is an automated system.

How enforcement plays out at the edge

1

Sender presents a certificate

Every outbound call, message, or automated request carries a certificate issued through the global CA hierarchy and an ISP sub-CA, including a disclosed attestation of whether the sender is a bot, a verified business system, or a human.

2

Gateway checks the attestation and revocation status

The ISP-issued home gateway (modem, cellular gateway, cable box) checks the certificate's trust tier and revocation status centrally, the same way it would for any sender — no extra hardware required in the home.

3

Undisclosed or unverifiable bot traffic is flagged or dropped

Automated traffic that fails to disclose itself as a bot, or that presents no valid certificate at all, is treated the same as spoofed traffic under the fail-closed model — flagged, deprioritized, or dropped depending on the trust tier the receiver has chosen.

4

Abusive bot certificates get revoked network-wide

A bot certificate that crosses the abuse threshold — spam volume, fraud complaints, scraping violations — gets added to the shared cross-ISP revoked-sender database, so it's blocked everywhere at once rather than ISP by ISP.

Before and after

Without a Universal Identity certificate

  • Bots and humans are indistinguishable at the network layer
  • No standard way to disclose automated origin
  • Bad actors re-register and reappear instantly after being blocked
  • Enforcement is reactive and platform-by-platform

With a Universal Identity certificate

  • Every sender carries a disclosed, verifiable trust tier
  • Receivers can choose to filter or deprioritize undisclosed automation
  • Revocation is shared across ISPs, closing the re-registration loophole
  • Enforcement happens at the edge, before traffic reaches the receiver

Extending the framework to AI bot traffic

This builds directly on the STIR-SHAKEN-style attestation and revocation model already proposed for calls and messages — extending trust-tier attestation to explicitly cover disclosed automated senders. Get involved or read the full technical framework.
